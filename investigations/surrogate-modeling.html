Can a neural net emulate the v2ecoli baseline? Four studies with pbg-torch: collect an 8-seed rollout dataset, train a surrogate and test it against trivial baselines (persistence, mean-delta, linear) under leave-one-trajectory-out CV, weigh the linear emulator's amortized break-even against the simulator, and try multi-step rollout-loss training.

passed Current verdict. Held to trivial baselines under 8-fold cross-validation, the neural surrogate does not justify itself: one-step R²≈1.000 is a persistence artifact, and in multi-step rollout a linear model is the best and most stable emulator of coarse growth (nRMSE 0.019, 0/8 unstable) while the neural net is no better and explodes on 2/8 folds. The broad 11.5k-observable panel is not learnable from the observable view alone (0% of observables beat persistence); it needs the cell's hidden state. The useful by-product is a cheap linear emulator that pays for its training data after ~8 evaluations and runs a 5,000-trajectory sweep in 0.02 s (vs ~24 h on the WCM).

Question. Can a neural-network surrogate, trained on v2ecoli baseline rollouts, emulate the model's per-step dynamics across a broad observable panel (growth/mass, exchange fluxes, metabolic fluxes, protein and transcript abundance, chromosome state), and does it beat trivial baselines enough to justify the complexity?

Hypothesis. We expected a residual-MLP surrogate (via pbg-torch) to emulate the smooth low-dimensional observables (mass, growth) well, with the high-dimensional vectors as the hard part. Held to trivial baselines under cross-validation, that did not survive: the neural net does not beat a linear model on the smooth target, and the high-dimensional panel is not learnable from the observable view at all.

🧬 Biology — the mechanism this investigation models The v2ecoli whole-cell model integrates 55 processes to advance one cell by one second. A surrogate asks whether a learned function can predict the next observable state…

The v2ecoli whole-cell model integrates 55 processes to advance one cell by one second. A surrogate asks whether a learned function can predict the next observable state directly and cheaply. The answer is split: the cell's coarse growth is smooth enough that a linear model emulates it better than a neural net, while its high-dimensional molecular state is governed by hidden variables a curated observable view does not carry, so it cannot be predicted from that view at all. The value is knowing which is which, and having a cheap emulator for the part that is emulable.

🔬 Scientific argument 2 for · 1 against Under cross-validation against trivial baselines, a neural surrogate does not beat a linear model at emulating the baseline's smooth observables, and…

Main claim. Under cross-validation against trivial baselines, a neural surrogate does not beat a linear model at emulating the baseline's smooth observables, and the high-dimensional cell state is not predictable from a curated observable view. The useful deliverable is a cheap linear emulator of coarse growth, not a neural net.

Evidence for

  • 8-fold CV: linear rollout nRMSE 0.019 (0/8 unstable) is best, beating the neural net (0.032, 2/8 explode) and persistence (1.850).
  • The cheap emulator pays back its 8 training sims after ~8 evaluations; a 5,000-trajectory sweep runs in 0.02 s vs ~24 h on the WCM.

Evidence against

  • One-step R²≈1.000 looks like strong emulation but is a persistence artifact (persistence also scores 1.000), not learned dynamics.

Caveats

  • Trained on 8 single-generation local trajectories; the cheap emulator covers coarse, smooth observables only.
  • The negative result is conditioned on this observable panel and modest data; sm-03 tests whether better training narrows the gap.

Setupcode/utility_demo.py fits the linear emulator on the compact dataset, sweeps 5,000 initial conditions × 250 steps (timed), and computes the amortized cost: (8 training sims) + N·(emulator rollout) vs N·(WCM rollout), giving the break-even N. Simulator per-step time is the measured baseline rate.
Result- Linear emulator: 5,000 × 250-step trajectories in 0.02 s (~0.005 ms/traj). - Full WCM: ~17 s per 250-step trajectory → the same sweep ≈ 24 h. - Per-trajectory speedup ≈ 3.8e6×; break-even after ~8 evaluations (i.e. the emulator pays back its 8 training sims almost immediately). Limits (from sm-01): the emulator is valid only for the smooth, low-dim observables; the broad 11.5k-observable panel is unlearnable from the observable view (0% beat persistence), and emulation says nothing about events outside the training distribution (e.g. division — a documented follow-up).
InterpretationThe expensive part of surrogate modeling is generating training data, not the emulator. Once you've paid for a handful of baseline simulations, a cheap emulator of a smooth observable is worth building even when a neural net adds nothing — it turns a 24-hour sweep into 0.02 s. The discipline is to scope it to what's actually emulable and to amortize honestly.
DecisionPASS — the cheap emulator amortizes after ~8 evaluations and its limits are stated explicitly.

Setupcode/rollout_train.py trains a pbg-torch SurrogateNet with a differentiable K=10-step rollout loss and compares it head-to-head against the one-step net and the linear baseline under the same 8-fold leave-one-trajectory-out CV (cell_mass rollout nRMSE + instability count).
Result8-fold CV, cell_mass rollout nRMSE (median) / unstable folds: - neural net, one-step loss : 0.069 (1/8 unstable) - neural net, rollout loss : 0.053 (0/8 unstable) <- improved + stabilized - linear baseline : 0.019 (0/8 unstable) <- still best Rollout-loss training removes the instability entirely and improves rollout accuracy by ~23%, but does not overtake the linear model.
InterpretationThe diagnosis was correct and the fix works: rollout-loss training is the right way to stabilize an autoregressive neural surrogate, and it measurably improves it. For a target as smooth as coarse cell mass, though, a linear model still wins — there is little nonlinear structure for the net to exploit. The value of this result is methodological: rollout-loss is the technique to apply to genuinely nonlinear targets (where a linear model fails), and the pipeline now supports it. It does not change sm-01's conclusion for THIS target; it shows the path for harder ones.
DecisionPASS — rollout-loss training narrows the gap (stabilizes + improves the net). The honest ranking for coarse growth is unchanged (linear best), but the follow-up establishes the right tool for harder targets.
